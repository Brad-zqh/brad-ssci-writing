# Section roles

Use the existing manuscript structure unless restructuring is requested. The patterns below describe rhetorical jobs, not a mandatory section count, word count, or journal template. Verify current venue rules when asked to check compliance.

## Abstract

Connect the problem and gap to the study design, the principal findings, and one supported implication. Keep the inventory of data and methods subordinate to the research question. Report only results actually established in the paper, with the right population and denominator.

Choose a few decisive findings rather than a full results catalog. A short transition such as “Several findings emerged” is acceptable if the author wants it. Do not delete it solely to follow a personal preference for maximal compression.

Define an unfamiliar measure compactly. Do not introduce “qualifying options” without enough context to understand what qualifies. If several conditions have already been explained, avoid listing them a second time.

## Introduction

Develop the real-world importance, the specific research tension, prior progress, the remaining question, and the study's response. Explain why the setting matters analytically, not simply that data are available there.

A gap should follow from the literature just reviewed. Distinguish:

- What the concept includes and what a dominant definition overlooks.
- What a measure observes and what it leaves unobserved.
- What a cross-sectional study can show and what requires comparable repeated observations.
- Whether social or spatial differences have already been studied, and what remains unclear when constraints are considered together.

Do not claim a whole literature ignores consumers merely because selected indices emphasize provision. Do not claim there are no setting-specific assessments when examples are already cited.

Introduce the actual objectives and the corresponding conceptual, empirical, or practical contributions. Do not inflate objectives into established findings or rehearse every processing step.

## Literature review

Organize around questions and concepts rather than one paragraph per citation. A useful progression is definition → relevant theoretical perspectives → measurement approaches → comparative evidence → a focused unresolved issue. Adapt it to the paper.

Compare studies along the dimensions relevant to the argument: construct, unit of analysis, setting, demand or supply perspective, time span, and what is inferred. Recognize genuine prior progress before identifying its boundary.

Separate concept-level gaps from index-construction gaps. If the argument concerns how an activity should be conceptualized, explain the missing theoretical perspective rather than simply proposing another score.

The conceptual framework can be the last literature-review subsection when it synthesizes the reviewed foundations. Determine its number from the actual outline and synchronize all downstream locations; do not hard-code a particular subsection number.

## Conceptual framework

For each construct, explain four things as needed:

1. Its substantive meaning.
2. Its established theoretical or empirical foundation.
3. Its relevance to the activity or setting under study.
4. Its relationship to the other perspectives represented in the framework.

For a supply–demand perspective, identify the mechanism connecting what is offered to what users select, expect, accept, or experience. Supply does not become demand simply because a customer-related dataset is available. Preferences and recorded experiences are not interchangeable; do not pretend that every dimension directly measures both sides.

Keep proxy extraction, prompts, frequency weighting, score bounds, aggregation, and missing-data rules in Methods or the supplement. Refer to operational details later. Avoid ending every construct paragraph by restating “This dimension therefore considers…”.

## Methods

Explain design, scope, data provenance, constructs and operational definitions, analysis, and validation in reader order. A data paragraph should say what was obtained from which source and why each source is needed. Prefer “We obtained…” or “We collected…” when that is the author's chosen voice. Use substantive bridges such as “For the population comparison…” rather than an ornamental connector before every source.

Introduce each equation's purpose before the formula and define symbols and units where the reader needs them. Distinguish annual observations, cumulative records, current attributes applied retrospectively, and population data reused across years.

Parameter sources, transformations, and value selection must be reproducible. Concise main-text wording may point to detailed supporting material; it must not hide an unsupported parameter or method change.

## Results

Lead with the substantive pattern, quantify the decisive comparison, and identify the relevant figure or table. Specify the direction, group, year, outcome, units, and denominator needed to interpret a result. Do not replace the result with “Figure X shows…”.

Keep literature comparisons and broader theoretical interpretation mainly in Discussion. Do not infer individual behavior from area-level conditions or household affordability from a generic recorded price band. Distinguish observed results from conditional scenario estimates.

## Discussion and theoretical contributions

Synthesize findings rather than copying the Results section. Where useful, connect the finding to prior evidence, explain the agreement or difference, and identify what the comparison changes in understanding.

For a claimed contribution, state the relevant prior boundary, the actual analytical advance, and the insight it enables. Several methods or more data do not by themselves demonstrate a theoretical advance. A temporal contribution should explain what changing provision, services, or preferences makes invisible in a single snapshot, not just announce more years.

Keep conceptual, empirical, methodological, and practical advances distinct. First/Second/Third can organize parallel contributions, but use only the number the research supports.

## Practical implications

Prefer one principal stakeholder per paragraph, with a finding-linked decision and a feasible action. Use a small set of actors that plausibly control the decisions. Avoid long actor lists that make recommendations appear broad but leave responsibility unclear.

If location optimization or scenario comparisons were actually added, explain how they strengthen decision support. Keep a hypothetical candidate site distinct from confirmed premises and simulated coverage distinct from an implemented benefit. Do not add an institution's mandate, a subsidy scheme, or regulatory feasibility without evidence.

## Limitations and conclusion

Limitations should identify a substantive boundary and, where useful, a feasible next study. Avoid a defensive inventory, but retain uncertainty that affects interpretation.

The conclusion answers the research question and states the supported contribution. Do not introduce a new result, source, method, or policy promise. Keep its role distinct from an exhaustive abstract repeated at the end.
