# Paragraph logic and language

## Diagnose the link before choosing a connector

Read adjacent sentences as a small argument. Ask: what did sentence A establish, what does sentence B add, and what premise lets the reader move between them?

- A definition can lead to a consequence only after its relevance to the study setting is explained.
- Evidence of variation can motivate comparison; it does not by itself demonstrate the cause of that variation.
- A limitation in an existing measure should lead to the specific aspect that remains unobserved, not directly to praise of a new index.
- The presence of a service is not the same as an opportunity to use it. Explain the applicable quality, price, or travel constraints when they matter.
- A static assessment can motivate repeated observations when provision, services, or preferences change. Explain what a single observation cannot distinguish.

An explicit connector is useful when it names a relationship already established by the content. “However” introduces an actual contrast; “therefore” marks an inference that has its premises; “for example” illustrates rather than extends a claim. Repeating a noun or using a clear pronoun can create a better link than adding another transition.

## Paragraph development

Give each paragraph a dominant purpose. A useful default is point → explanation → evidence or illustration → implication. Adapt the order to the rhetorical task. Do not manufacture a concluding sentence if it only repeats the topic sentence.

Keep sentence subjects stable enough to follow. Prefer an intelligible chain such as provision → available options → user choices → experienced outcome. Avoid jumping from a literature finding to a data source, then to an operational formula, within a theoretical paragraph.

If the author says “too short,” first identify the missing relationship or explanation. Add that material, not a longer list of synonyms. If the author says “too long,” remove duplicated work before removing premises.

## Control repetition at three levels

1. **Within a sentence:** do not repeat “long-term” or “social inequality” on both sides of a purpose clause when the second occurrence adds nothing.
2. **Across adjacent sentences:** define the criteria once, then use “these conditions,” “these options,” or another unambiguous reference.
3. **Across paragraphs:** vary the development and ending. “First,” “Second,” and “Third” are useful for parallel contributions, but the paragraphs do not all need the same concluding formula.

Do not solve repetition by substituting less precise synonyms for a technical term. Consistent construct names are helpful. Repetition is a problem when the argument repeats, not merely when a necessary term recurs.

## Concrete and restrained English

- Prefer a concrete subject and action: “Fees restrict the available choices” rather than “The issue of affordability exerts a multidimensional influence.”
- Use plain research verbs such as examine, compare, trace, estimate, assess, and identify. Choose one that fits what was actually done.
- Avoid stacked compounds and opaque labels that need more explanation than they save.
- Prefer “previous studies” when a geographic label for a cited study adds no analytical information. Retain the location when it matters to transferability, comparison, or the literature finding.
- State a limited gap as limited evidence, incomplete integration, or an unexamined relationship. Do not turn “less attention” into “no studies.”
- Avoid mechanical lists of fashionable phrases: “comprehensive,” “holistic,” “novel paradigm,” “robust framework,” and “deeper insights” need a concrete referent and evidence.

There is no universal banned-word list. “Therefore,” “yet,” “reveal,” “framework,” passive voice, and a short findings transition can all be appropriate. Judge the sentence's job and the author's choice.

## Non-defensive, not underqualified

Reader-facing prose describes the construct, analysis, result, or limitation. It does not repeatedly reassure an imagined reviewer.

Remove empty formulas such as “to avoid misunderstanding” when the following sentence can directly state the relevant definition. Do not repeatedly announce what a measure is not after its scope has been explained once.

Retain distinctions that change interpretation: an association is not an identified causal effect; a recorded charge is not a household-specific affordability measure; a review mention is not a verified transaction; a scenario is not an implemented intervention. Put the distinction in the relevant definition or limitation, then use the measure consistently.

Reviewer-directed process language belongs in a response letter, not in the manuscript. A response letter may accurately say “We clarified…” or “We added…” without making the revised manuscript defensive.

## Bilingual and author-facing delivery

Use the language requested for the deliverable. Chinese discussion does not authorize adding Chinese text to an English submission. If bilingual pairing is requested, preserve paragraph alignment and technical meaning. If the author requests only revised English, return only that passage.

Explain substantive concerns outside the manuscript. Do not add an internal audit note, missing-source warning, or account of editing decisions to the publication text.

## Apply a local correction without losing the surrounding argument

Before returning a revised passage, read its opening and ending against the neighboring paragraph when that context is available. Check pronoun referents, the scope of “these,” whether a contrast was actually established, and whether a reordered citation still supports the same proposition.

For recurring specific complaints, use [Micro-editing decisions](micro-editing.md): repairing a suspended “while” clause, compressing a duplicated purpose, unpacking an opaque opportunity label, reducing repeated criteria, preserving accepted signposts, and choosing the source-attribution voice the author actually requested. Do not mistake a shorter standalone sentence for a better-connected paragraph.
